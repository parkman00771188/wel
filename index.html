Our Mission

Turn seismic data into knowledge, and knowledge into resilience.

The agencies that record earthquakes publish their catalogues openly, but reading them takes tools most people do not have. This site merges those catalogues, keeps them current, and puts the map, the analysis and the background in one browser tab — free, with no account and nothing to install.

The Platform

One workspace for the world's earthquake catalogues.

Six views over one merged catalogue. The archive back to 1900 is rebuilt offline and shipped as compressed binary, so a century of seismicity can be scrubbed at speed; the recent window is re-fetched from the USGS every ten minutes. No account, no installation, no licence — all of it runs in the browser.

A single screen that answers "what is happening right now?" — event counts and trends for any period from 24 hours to 10 years, magnitude distribution, the largest events, a live global map, and the sources behind all of it.

Live Map

Every earthquake since 1900 — on a map you can play.

The live map is two instruments in one. In 2D, a fast interactive world map for browsing recent seismicity; in 3D, the Earthquake 4D engine — a globe that replays the entire catalog through time and shows every hypocenter at its true depth.

Pick any window with the start–end date controls — the last 24 hours, a famous sequence from decades ago, or all 126 years at once — and both views follow. Press play and events light up in order; drag the time bar to scrub back and forth.

  • Any date range, 1900 → today. Presets from 24 hours to 10 years, or type exact dates.
  • 2D basemaps — topographic, satellite with night tint, ocean, gray, and streets — plus plate-boundary overlays.
  • The 4D globe renders depth in 3D: subducting slabs and deep seismic zones appear as real structures.
  • Layers & filters — magnitude bands, depth ranges, active faults, volcanoes, coloring by depth, magnitude, time, or density.

Earthquake Guide

Understand the shaking — then prepare for it.

A plain-language guide to earthquake science and safety, organized by topic so you can read the piece you need in a few minutes.

  • Basics — what an earthquake is, hypocenter vs epicenter, and how P and S waves make early warning possible.
  • Plate tectonics — convergent, divergent, and transform boundaries, and why 90% of quakes trace the Ring of Fire.
  • Magnitude vs intensity — the logarithmic scale, ×32 energy per step, and the M 2–9 class table with worldwide frequencies.
  • Key terms & FAQ — aftershocks, liquefaction, tsunamis; can quakes be predicted, and how alerts work.

The Safety Guide turns knowledge into habits: what to do indoors, outdoors, while driving, or in an elevator — each as a three-step drill — plus an interactive emergency-kit checklist that remembers your progress, and a three-point home readiness check.

Seismic Insights

Trends, spectra, and hotspots — for any window you choose.

Six chart families recomputed on demand from the same catalog that drives the map, from a single day to a full decade, globally or per region.

  • Activity over time with rolling averages — hourly, daily, or monthly bins depending on the window.
  • A real Gutenberg–Richter curve computed from the full catalog, M 2 to M 9.5.
  • Depth distribution and seismic energy release, log-scaled so great earthquakes stay readable.
  • Regional hotspots ranked by event count, and an activity-anomaly chart comparing observed counts against a rolling baseline — a signal worth looking at, not a prediction.
  • One-click CSV export of whatever selection you are looking at.

Research Hub

The literature, and where every number here comes from.

Two things, both of them checkable. A reading list that keeps itself current, and a plain statement of the sources behind the maps and the charts.

  • Publications, collected automatically. OpenAlex topic T13018, Seismology and Earthquake Studies, widened a page at a time on the same cycle as the earthquake data. Sort by citations, newest, oldest or title.
  • Data sources, named. The eight catalogues, reference layers and feeds this site actually reads, each linking to the original rather than to a copy, with the USGS and ISC row counts read from the archive metadata rather than typed in.
  • No original research is published here. This is a place to read the field and to see the provenance of what is on screen.

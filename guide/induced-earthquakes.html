Induced Earthquakes

Earthquakes people cause

A fault fails when the stress driving it exceeds the friction holding it. Pump fluid into the rock, pile a reservoir on top, or take gas out from underneath, and the balance can tip decades earlier than nature would have managed.

By the World Earthquake Labs team · Checked against the USGS and peer-reviewed references listed at the end · Published September 2026

How a fault is pushed over the edge

Rock deep in the crust is full of water under pressure, and that pressure works against the weight of the rock above. What holds a fault shut is the difference between the two, the effective stress. Raise the fluid pressure inside the fault, even slightly, and the clamping force falls; if the fault was already close to slipping under tectonic stress, it slips. Nothing about the injection supplies the energy of the earthquake. The energy was stored by plate motion over centuries. The injection changes the timing.

The pressure does not have to reach the fault directly. Fluid pumped into a porous layer spreads for kilometres over months and years, and the pressure front can reach a basement fault far from the well and long after pumping began. Removing fluid works the other way: the rock compacts, stresses around the emptied volume shift, and faults at its edges can fail. A reservoir does both at once, loading the crust with the weight of the water and raising pore pressure as it seeps down.

Seismologists distinguish induced earthquakes, where the human stress change is a large part of the total, from triggered ones, where a small change tips a fault that was going to fail anyway. In practice the line is blurred, and the word "induced" is used for both. What matters for the people above is the same: an earthquake that would not have happened then, or there, without the operation.

The main causes

Almost every way of putting fluid into the ground or taking material out of it has produced earthquakes somewhere. Most are far too small to feel. A few have been damaging.

"Disputed" marks cases where a human cause has been argued seriously but not settled. The largest earthquakes in the table are still far smaller than the great natural earthquakes; the concern is that they happen under towns that were never built for any shaking at all.

Oklahoma: a state that became seismic

Before 2009 Oklahoma recorded one or two earthquakes of magnitude 3 or more in a typical year. In 2015 it recorded close to nine hundred, more than California. The cause was not the hydraulic fracturing that made the headlines but the disposal of the salt water that comes up with the oil: billions of litres a year injected into the Arbuckle formation, which sits directly on the ancient faulted basement. The pressure found the faults.

The largest was the M 5.8 near Pawnee in September 2016, the strongest ever recorded in the state; an M 5.7 near Prague in 2011 and an M 5.0 at Cushing, beneath one of the world's largest oil storage hubs, framed it. From 2015 the state regulator ordered injection volumes cut across the affected area, and the rate of earthquakes fell year on year, to a few dozen M 3 events annually by the 2020s. The USGS, for the first time, published a one-year hazard forecast that included induced earthquakes, and for 2016 it put parts of Oklahoma on a par with California.

Pohang: the earthquake that closed a power plant

On 15 November 2017 an M 5.4 earthquake struck Pohang on Korea's south-east coast, the second-largest ever recorded in the country by instruments after Gyeongju the year before. More than a hundred people were injured, well over a thousand were displaced, a university entrance exam was postponed nationwide for the first time, and the damage ran to hundreds of billions of won.

Two kilometres from the epicentre stood a pilot enhanced geothermal plant that had injected water at high pressure into 4-km-deep wells during 2016 and 2017, and had recorded small earthquakes after each injection. A government commission with international members concluded in March 2019 that the injections had triggered the earthquake by raising pressure on a fault that had not been identified. The plant was permanently closed, the site is being restored, and the case became the reference for how a modest injection can trigger an earthquake far larger than the volume alone would suggest.

Groningen: the slow one

The Groningen field in the northern Netherlands is Europe's largest gas field, produced since 1963. The first felt earthquake came in 1991, nearly thirty years in, as the reservoir rock compacted and the faults above it began to move. The largest, M 3.6 at Huizinge in August 2012, would pass unnoticed in Japan. In Groningen it cracked tens of thousands of unreinforced brick houses standing on soft soil, and it started a decade of damage claims, protests and parliamentary inquiry. Production was cut from 2014 and stopped in 2023, and the field has been closed permanently. The earthquakes have not entirely stopped, because the compaction that drives them lags the extraction by years.

Groningen is the counter-example to Oklahoma: small earthquakes, no injection, a slow mechanism, and yet a national crisis, because the buildings were never designed for any shaking and the ground beneath them amplified what little there was. Which is a point the buildings chapter makes from the other direction.

Telling induced from natural

No single earthquake carries a label. The case is made statistically, with a set of questions that seismologists have used since the 1990s. Were there earthquakes here before the operation began? Did they start soon after it did, and do they rise and fall with the injection volume or pressure? Are they within a few kilometres of the well, at a depth the pressure could reach? Is there a plausible fault, and a plausible path to it? A "yes" to all of these is persuasive; a "no" to any of them is not a proof of innocence, because pressure travels and faults hide.

Regulators have turned that into traffic-light protocols: injection continues at green, slows at amber, and stops at red, with the thresholds set by local law. Britain set red at magnitude 0.5, the strictest in the world; Alberta sets it at 4.0. The protocol at Pohang was triggered several times before the main event, which is part of why the case is studied.

What it means for reading the map

Catalogues do not flag induced earthquakes; the agencies locate them and leave the attribution to research. So a reader of the live map has to recognise the signature. A tight cluster of shallow events, 2 to 8 km deep, in the stable interior of a continent far from any plate boundary, with a starting date and sometimes a stopping date, is usually a human story: the central United States, the north of the Netherlands, the coalfields of Silesia, the geothermal fields of California and Iceland.

The catalogue chapter warns that a rising count of small earthquakes usually means a growing network. Oklahoma is the exception that proves the rule: the network did not change, the earthquakes did, and the reason was underground.

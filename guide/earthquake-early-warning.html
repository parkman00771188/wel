Early Warning

Seconds of warning, and where they come from

An alert that arrives before the shaking feels like prediction. It is the opposite. The earthquake has already begun; the warning is simply faster than the waves.

By the World Earthquake Labs team · Checked against the USGS, JMA and Google references listed at the end · Published September 2026

The physics of a head start

An earthquake sends out two kinds of body wave. The P wave, a compression, travels through the crust at roughly 6 km per second and does little damage. The S wave, a shear, follows at about 3.5 km per second and, together with the surface waves behind it, carries almost all of the destructive motion. A seismometer near the epicentre feels the P wave first; a computer recognises it, and a message goes out by radio and fibre at the speed of light. Everywhere beyond a certain distance, that message arrives before the S wave does.

The arithmetic is simple. Detection takes a few seconds, because the system waits for several stations to agree before it believes an earthquake. After that, every 3.5 km of distance from the source buys about one more second. The table gives the rough figures.

Assuming about five seconds to detect and issue. Real systems vary, and shaking from a large earthquake begins before the S wave of its first patch of rupture arrives at distant points, so these are upper bounds.

The region inside the first 20 or 30 km gets no warning at all. That is the blind zone, and it is the fundamental limit of the method: the place that shakes hardest is the place the message cannot reach in time. Subduction zones are the exception that makes the idea worthwhile. Their great earthquakes begin offshore, a hundred kilometres or more from the coast, and the cities that matter are far enough away to gain tens of seconds.

How the system decides

From the first few seconds of P wave at the nearest stations, the system estimates where the earthquake is and roughly how big it is, predicts the shaking intensity at every place it serves, and issues an alert wherever the prediction crosses a threshold. It has to do all of this faster than a person could read the numbers, and it has to be right often enough that people keep listening.

The hard part is size. A magnitude 5 rupture is over in a second or two; a magnitude 9 keeps rupturing for two or three minutes, and nothing in the first seconds reveals how far it will go. In 2011 the Japanese system issued its first alert less than ten seconds after the Tōhoku earthquake began, with an estimate near M 8. The earthquake ended at M 9.0. The alert reached Sendai and the coast in time; in Tokyo, 370 km away, the predicted intensity was too low and no public warning was given for shaking that turned out to be strong. Newer methods watch the rupture as it grows, using dense strong-motion networks and GNSS receivers that measure the ground's permanent displacement, and update the estimate while the earthquake is still happening.

Systems around the world

Japan
The Japan Meteorological Agency has issued public earthquake early warnings since October 2007, over television, radio and a cell broadcast to every phone in the affected prefectures, when shaking of intensity 5-lower or more on the JMA scale is expected. Shinkansen trains brake automatically; lifts stop at the nearest floor; factory lines pause. It is the most comprehensive system in operation.
Mexico
SASMEX, the oldest public system, has warned Mexico City since 1991 using sensors on the Guerrero coast about 300 km away, where the great subduction earthquakes begin. That geometry gives the capital about a minute. The 2017 Puebla earthquake, which began inland and much closer, showed the other side: the sirens sounded only seconds before the shaking.
United States
ShakeAlert, run by the USGS with state and university partners, covers California, Oregon and Washington. Public alerts began in California in October 2019 and in the other two states in 2021, delivered through Wireless Emergency Alerts, the MyShake app and Android.
Korea
The Korea Meteorological Administration issues early warnings for larger earthquakes to every phone in the country by cell broadcast. After the 2016 Gyeongju and 2017 Pohang earthquakes the target time from detection to alert was cut to a few seconds, and the alerts now arrive before the shaking in most of the country for an earthquake of that size.
Taiwan
The Central Weather Administration operates a dense strong-motion network that issues public warnings within about ten seconds, with alerts to phones and automatic actions in schools and on the high-speed railway.
Android phones
Since 2020, Google has used the accelerometers in Android phones as a crowd-sourced seismic network. Phones that feel a sudden motion report it; when enough of them agree, alerts are sent to phones in the area about to shake. Where an official system exists, as on the US west coast, the phone relays that instead. The service now runs in dozens of countries that have no ground network of their own.
Others
Chile, Israel, Türkiye, Romania, China, India's Uttarakhand and several other regions operate or are building systems, most on the same principle and most still limited to a few cities or a single fault.

What can be done with ten seconds

More than it sounds. Most injuries in an earthquake come from falling objects and from people moving while the ground is shaking, and both are avoidable with a few seconds' notice. The personal response is the one the Safety Guide drills: drop, cover and hold on where you are, and do not run outside. A driver slows and pulls over. A surgeon lifts the scalpel. A teacher says one word and a class is under the desks.

The automatic responses are worth more, because they need no one to react. Trains brake, which is what kept the Shinkansen fleet on its rails in 2011. Lifts stop at a floor and open. Gas valves close, cranes lock, refinery processes go to a safe state, data centres finish writing to disk. None of these prevent the earthquake's damage; they take the worst of the consequences off the table.

Limits and false alarms

Every system has issued alerts for shaking that never came. The usual causes are two small earthquakes at the same moment being read as one large one, and a single noisy sensor being believed before the others could disagree. Japan's system did both in its first decade and tightened its rules each time. The opposite failure, an alert that comes too late or not at all, is built into the blind zone and into the slow growth of great ruptures.

Two things early warning is not. It is not prediction, which no agency can do; see the FAQ. And it is not a tsunami warning. Those come minutes later from the same agencies, based on the finished earthquake and on sea-level gauges, and carry their own grades. If you are on the coast and the shaking is strong or long, do not wait for either.

The live map on this site shows an earthquake minutes after it happens, once an agency has catalogued it. That is a record, not an alert. For warnings, turn on your phone's emergency alerts and, where one exists, install the official app for your region.

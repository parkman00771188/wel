Notable Earthquakes

The largest, the deadliest, and what they changed

The two lists barely overlap. Nearly every advance in earthquake safety was bought by one of these.

What each disaster changed

Nearly every advance in earthquake safety was bought by a catastrophe. The pattern is consistent enough to be worth stating plainly: the science improves, the codes follow, and the next earthquake in that place kills far fewer people.

1906
San Francisco. Reid's study of the offset fences and roads along the San Andreas gave us elastic rebound — the first correct physical theory of what an earthquake is.
1923
Kantō. Japan introduced the world's first national seismic design requirement the following year, beginning a century of code development.
1960
Chile. The tsunami killed people in Hawaii and Japan a day after the shaking, which made a Pacific-wide warning system unarguable.
1964
Alaska and Niigata. Alaska's data helped confirm plate tectonics; Niigata, the same year, made liquefaction a standard part of geotechnical engineering.
1985
Mexico City. Catastrophic damage 350 km from the rupture proved that local soil can matter more than distance, and site amplification entered codes worldwide.
2004
Indian Ocean. An ocean with no warning system got one, along with sustained investment in coastal evacuation planning and education.
2011
Tōhoku. Hazard models had not allowed for an M 9 on that margin. The lesson — that the largest event in the historical record is not the largest possible — reshaped how maximum credible earthquakes are estimated everywhere.
The 2010 Chile earthquake is the counter-example worth remembering. At M 8.8 it released about 500 times the energy of Haiti's M 7.0 six weeks earlier, and killed roughly a five-hundredth as many people. Construction standards, not luck.

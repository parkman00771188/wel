Buildings & Codes

Buildings decide the toll

Two earthquakes six weeks apart in 2010 said it plainly. An M 7.0 in Haiti killed well over a hundred thousand people. An M 8.8 in Chile, hundreds of times more energetic, killed about five hundred. The difference was construction.

The same shaking, different outcomes

Magnitude is a poor predictor of deaths. What the ground was, what the buildings were made of, and whether anyone had enforced a code explain far more. Death tolls below are given as ranges where sources disagree.

Chile and Haiti are the textbook pair. The Chilean code, rewritten after the 1960 Valdivia earthquake and again after 1985, requires stiff shear walls in every direction; buildings are designed to be damaged and stay standing. Haiti had no enforced code at all, and the concrete in much of Port-au-Prince was mixed with too little cement and too much sand.

What shaking does to a building

The ground moves sideways under a building and the building's own mass resists, so the structure is loaded as if pushed by a horizontal force proportional to its weight. Gravity design never planned for that. Every storey adds mass above the ground floor, and the ground floor's columns have to carry all of it sideways.

Buildings also have a natural period, the time they take to sway back and forth once, roughly a tenth of a second per storey. Ground shaking has its own dominant periods, set by the earthquake and, above all, by the soil. When the two match, the building resonates and the motion grows with every cycle. In Mexico City in 1985 the soft clay of the old lake bed rang at about two seconds, the period of buildings between six and fifteen storeys. Those collapsed in their hundreds while low houses and the tallest towers, tuned to other periods, largely survived. Soft soil amplifies shaking in general, sometimes several-fold, which is why the hazards chapter keeps returning to the ground rather than the magnitude.

Duration matters too. A building that survives twenty seconds of strong shaking may not survive two minutes of it, because each cycle works loose a little more of what holds it together. Great subduction earthquakes shake for minutes; a crustal M 6 is over in ten seconds.

The ways buildings fail

Unreinforced masonry
Brick, block, stone or adobe held together only by mortar has almost no strength in tension. Walls crack, then peel away from floors and roofs, then fall into the street. It is the deadliest building type in the world and still the most common.
Soft storey
An open ground floor for shops or parking, with walls above it, concentrates all the sideways motion in one weak level. The floors above stay rigid and drop as a block. Most photographs of a building that has "sunk" one floor show this.
Non-ductile concrete
Concrete frames built before modern detailing, with too few ties around the steel, fail suddenly at the joints. Floors then fall onto floors, the pancake collapse in which there are almost no survivable spaces.
Short columns
A column partly braced by a wall or a window sill is stiff only over its free height, and takes far more force than it was designed for. Common in schools with strip windows.
Pounding
Neighbouring buildings of different heights sway out of step and hammer each other, typically at the level where one building's floor meets the other's column mid-height.
Non-structural damage
Parapets, facades, ceilings, glass, shelves and water heaters. In moderate earthquakes these cause most injuries even when the structure is sound, which is the reason the Safety Guide spends so long on securing contents.

What a code actually asks for

A seismic code does not promise an undamaged building. Its baseline objective is life safety: in the largest shaking the site is expected to see, the building may be ruined, but it must not collapse on the people inside. Hospitals, fire stations and schools are designed to a higher standard so they remain usable afterwards.

The means are a short list that engineers have refined after every disaster. Ductility: members that bend and absorb energy rather than snap, achieved in concrete by closely spaced steel ties that confine the core. Strong column, weak beam: beams are designed to yield first, so that damage spreads across a frame instead of taking out a column. Shear walls and braced frames that give a building stiffness in both directions. A continuous load path from roof to foundation, with every connection able to pass the force along. And regularity: no soft storeys, no abrupt changes in stiffness, no heavy masses up high.

Codes are national and they date from a country's own disasters. Japan's decisive revision came in 1981; buildings from before it are the ones its retrofit programmes target. California rewrote its rules after 1933, 1971 and 1994. Chile's after 1960 and 1985. Korea introduced seismic design for larger buildings in 1988 and has extended it to nearly all new construction since, with the 2016 Gyeongju and 2017 Pohang earthquakes accelerating the last steps. In every case the year a building was permitted tells you which rules it was built to.

Beyond the code: isolation and damping

The most effective idea in earthquake engineering is not to make a building stronger but to disconnect it from the ground. A base-isolated building rests on bearings of layered rubber and steel, often with a lead core, that let the ground slide back and forth beneath it while the structure above moves slowly and gently. Japan has thousands of isolated buildings, including most new hospitals; the retrofits of San Francisco City Hall and Los Angeles City Hall put entire historic structures onto isolators. Christchurch Women's Hospital, isolated, kept working through 2011 while buildings around it were condemned.

Dampers absorb energy the way a car's shock absorbers do: viscous cylinders in a braced frame, or friction devices at the joints. A tuned mass damper is a huge pendulum near the top of a tower, swinging out of phase with the building; the 660-tonne sphere in Taipei 101 is the famous example, built mainly against typhoon winds but working in earthquakes as well. None of these are required by codes. They are chosen where the owner wants the building to keep functioning, not merely to keep standing.

Retrofitting what already exists

Codes govern new construction, and most of the risk is in buildings that already exist. That is why the largest programmes are retrofits. After Kobe, Japan passed a law promoting seismic retrofit of pre-1981 buildings and has been strengthening schools, hospitals and housing since. California required cities to inventory unreinforced masonry in 1986; Los Angeles ordered the retrofit of soft-storey apartment buildings in 2015 and of non-ductile concrete buildings on a longer timetable. Istanbul, Kathmandu, Lima and many other cities have inventories of what would fall and programmes, always under-funded, to fix it.

For a house the measures are modest and well known: bolt the frame to its foundation, brace the short "cripple" walls under the floor, strap the water heater, anchor tall furniture, and replace an unbraced masonry chimney. For a reader who wants to judge the building they are in, a few questions go a long way. When was it permitted, and what did the code say then? Is the ground floor open, with walls above? Is it brick without visible steel? Did the last earthquake leave diagonal cracks at the corners of openings? None of these substitute for an engineer, but they are what an engineer would look at first.

The intensity scales in Magnitude & Intensity are defined by what happens to buildings, which is why the same shaking earns a different intensity in a city of adobe and a city of steel. USGS ShakeMaps estimate the shaking, not the damage; the damage is what this chapter is about.

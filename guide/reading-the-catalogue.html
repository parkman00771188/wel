Reading the Catalogue

A record of what was detected, not of what happened

Every chart on this site is computed from a catalogue. Reading one well means knowing what its counts, magnitudes and depths can say, and what they cannot.

By the World Earthquake Labs team · Checked against the USGS and ISC documentation listed at the end · Published September 2026

Why the number of earthquakes keeps rising

Plot the yearly count of earthquakes in almost any catalogue and it climbs steeply through the twentieth century. The Earth did not become more active. The instruments did. The Worldwide Standardized Seismograph Network of the 1960s gave the first consistent global coverage; digital broadband stations spread in the 1990s; dense regional networks have been filling the gaps since. Each step lowered the size of the smallest earthquake that could be reliably recorded, and the count rose to match.

Seismologists call that threshold the magnitude of completeness, written Mc: the magnitude above which essentially every earthquake in a region is caught. Globally it has fallen from roughly M 7 at the start of the record to about M 5 by the late 1960s and somewhat below M 4.5 today. In densely instrumented regions such as California and Japan it is near M 1 or 2. In the oceans and much of Africa and Antarctica it is still far higher.

The practical rule follows directly. Compare two periods only above the completeness of the earlier one. A trend since 1900 is trustworthy at M 7 and above; a trend since the 1970s at about M 5; a trend in the count of M 3 events is usually a trend in the network. On the Insights page, the activity chart lets you set a minimum magnitude for exactly this reason.

One earthquake, several magnitudes

Agencies publish different magnitude types, measured from different parts of the seismogram, and the same earthquake will carry a different number in each. The differences are not errors; they are different questions being asked of the same waves.

"Saturates" means the scale stops growing even as the earthquake does: the waves it measures cannot get any bigger, so an M 8.5 and an M 9.2 look alike. Moment magnitude was introduced in the late 1970s to fix that, which is why the very largest events in old catalogues were re-estimated decades after they happened. Differences of a few tenths between agencies reporting the same type are normal; differences between types can exceed half a unit. This site keeps the type each source published, so a card that reads mb 5.9 and one that reads Mw 6.2 may be the same earthquake seen twice.

The first solution is not the last

Within minutes of an earthquake a computer has located it from the first stations to report and estimated a magnitude. That automatic solution is what news reports carry and what appears on the live map first. Over the following hours an analyst re-picks the arrivals with more stations, the depth is refined, and for anything large a moment magnitude replaces the rapid estimate. Revisions of 0.2 or 0.3 units are routine. Weeks later a regional network may publish its own, more precise, solution.

Some entries disappear. Two networks report the same event and one is removed; a quarry blast, a sonic boom or a mine collapse is recognised and reclassified; a false detection triggered by noise is deleted. That is why this site re-fetches the last fourteen days on every ten-minute cycle rather than only adding new events: withdrawals and corrections follow through. The status shown on an event card, Automatic or Reviewed, is the agency's own flag; where the source does not publish one, the card says so rather than guessing.

Depth is the weakest number

Latitude and longitude come from differences in arrival time across a network that surrounds the earthquake. Depth comes from much subtler differences, and if all the stations lie on one side, as they do for anything offshore, it is poorly constrained. When the data cannot resolve it, the analyst fixes depth at a default and publishes that. For decades the international default for shallow events was 33 km; today it is usually 10 km, sometimes 5 km. A depth histogram of any global catalogue therefore has sharp spikes at exactly those values, and they are artefacts, not layers in the Earth.

Depth becomes reliable when a station sits nearly above the earthquake, or when the analyst can identify depth phases: the P wave that first travelled up to the surface, reflected off it, and then set out to the distant station a few seconds behind the direct arrival. That delay measures depth almost directly, which is why deep earthquakes, 300 to 700 km down under Tonga or Japan, are often located more precisely in depth than shallow ones.

Where catalogues overlap, and how they are joined

This site reads three catalogues. Each is authoritative for something, and the same earthquake appears in more than one of them with slightly different time, place and size.

USGS ComCat
The Comprehensive Catalog gathers solutions from the US National Earthquake Information Center and contributing regional networks worldwide. For each event one contributor is authoritative, normally the network closest to it; the others are kept as alternatives. It is the fastest of the three and the one the live overlay reads.
ISC Bulletin
The International Seismological Centre collects readings from more than 150 agencies, relocates every event with all of them together, and publishes the result about two years after the fact. It is the most complete global record, and the reviewed part of it is what the archive here uses for the years it covers.
JMA
The Japan Meteorological Agency runs one of the densest networks anywhere and catalogues events far smaller than the global agencies can see. For Japan it is the reference; elsewhere it does not apply.
Joining them
When the archive is rebuilt, records that fall within a short window of time and distance of each other are treated as one event and a single record is kept. The number removed that way is reported on the data sources page. The matching can miss: a great earthquake whose origin time differs by several seconds between agencies, or two genuine events close together, may be kept as two or merged into one.
Location error
On land inside a dense network a location is good to a kilometre or two. Offshore, or before the 1960s, tens of kilometres is normal, and the error is larger in the direction where stations are missing. Catalogues publish this as an error ellipse and an azimuthal gap, the largest angle with no station in it; a gap above 180° is a warning sign.

Reading this site's charts with all that in mind

Long-term trends
Use M 7 and above for the full span since 1900, or M 5 and above from the mid-1960s. A rising count of small events is the network growing.
Regional counts
Before comparing two regions, or one region across decades, ask whether their networks are comparable. Japan's count is high partly because Japan listens harder.
Magnitude–frequency curves
The Gutenberg–Richter line should be straight with a slope, the b-value, near 1. Where it bends downward at the small end, that bend marks the completeness magnitude. Nothing below it is a fair count.
Depth profiles
Ignore the spikes at 10 km and 33 km. The genuine structure is the band of shallow crustal events and, under subduction zones, the inclined zone descending to 700 km.
Largest events
For anything before the 1970s the listed magnitude is a modern re-estimate of an old measurement. Valdivia 1960 is Mw 9.5 by moment; the surface-wave magnitude published at the time was 8.5.
The last two weeks
Numbers move. A magnitude quoted today may be a few tenths different next week, and a small event near a large one may vanish as a duplicate.
How the numbers are produced in the first place is the subject of Measuring Quakes; what the magnitude scale means for the energy released is in Magnitude & Intensity.

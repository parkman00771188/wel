What this site is

World Earthquake Labs brings together, in one place, the things a curious reader usually has to collect from several agencies and a stack of papers:

  • A live map of earthquakes in 2D and on a 3D globe, covering about 2.97 million events since 1900, filterable by magnitude, depth and date.
  • A dashboard of the latest activity: the largest recent events, counts over any window from 24 hours to the whole catalogue, magnitude and depth distributions.
  • Seismic insights: activity over time, a real Gutenberg–Richter curve computed from the full catalogue, depth profiles, energy release and regional hotspots, with CSV export.
  • An earthquake guide in nine parts, from what an earthquake is to plate tectonics, magnitude versus intensity, hazards, notable events and practical safety.
  • A research hub that tracks the seismology literature of the last ten years, and a news feed of earthquake reporting, both collected automatically.

Where the data comes from

Nothing on this site is typed in by hand. The earthquake catalogue is built from public, freely available sources and the site reads it directly:

  • USGS ANSS Comprehensive Catalog (ComCat): worldwide events of magnitude 2.0 and above. The most recent 14 days are fetched again every ten minutes, so revised magnitudes and withdrawn events are reflected here as well.
  • ISC Bulletin (International Seismological Centre): reviewed events of magnitude 3.0 and above, back to 1900.
  • Japan Meteorological Agency (JMA): Japan and its surrounding seas, including small events that no global catalogue publishes.

The two global catalogues are merged and de-duplicated offline into a single archive. Reference layers (active faults, volcanoes, plate boundaries, basemaps) and the publication metadata have their own sources, listed with links on the Data Sources page.

What it is not

World Earthquake Labs is an information and education site. It is not an official earthquake or tsunami warning service, it does not issue alerts, and it does not predict earthquakes. Nobody can. For warnings and safety instructions, follow your national or regional agency, for example the USGS and NOAA Tsunami Warning Centers in the United States, the Japan Meteorological Agency in Japan, or PHIVOLCS in the Philippines, and local civil-protection authorities.

Catalogue data is reported by the agencies as they publish it and can be revised for hours or days after an event. Our Terms of Service describe the limits of what the site can promise.

How it is funded

The site is free to use and has no accounts, subscriptions or paywalls. Running costs are covered by advertising served through Google AdSense. How that affects you and what data is involved is set out in the Privacy Policy.

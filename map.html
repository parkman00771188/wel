How to read the live earthquake map

Every dot is one earthquake from a catalogue of about 2.97 million events recorded since 1900. The map is a research instrument, not a warning service: it shows what the agencies have published, as soon as they publish it.

What a dot means

Each dot sits on the epicenter, the point on the surface directly above where the rupture began. Its size and colour follow magnitude, in nine steps from M 1 to M 9+. In the 3D view you can switch the colouring to depth or to time, and the globe plots each event at its true depth below the surface, so a subducting plate appears as a sloping sheet of hypocenters rather than a smear on the map.

A magnitude step is not a small difference. Each whole unit means about 32 times more energy released, so the single M 7 on a busy day carries more energy than all of that day's smaller earthquakes together.

Why the dots line up

Earthquakes are where plates meet. Turn on the plate boundaries layer and most dots fall within a few hundred kilometres of a line: the subduction zones around the Pacific, the mid-ocean ridges, the collision belt from the Mediterranean to the Himalaya. The Pacific rim alone produces the large majority of the world's seismic energy.

Deep earthquakes, below about 70 km, occur only inside cold slabs of oceanic plate sinking into the mantle. Filter the map to depths over 300 km and what remains is a map of the world's subduction zones, and nothing else.

What "live" means here, and where the data comes from

Three catalogues feed this map. The USGS ANSS Comprehensive Catalog supplies worldwide events of magnitude 2.0 and above, and its most recent 14 days are fetched again every ten minutes, so an earthquake usually appears here within a quarter of an hour of the USGS publishing it. The ISC Bulletin supplies the reviewed record back to 1900; because the ISC publishes about two years behind real time, the recent tail of the archive is USGS data. The Japan Meteorological Agency supplies Japan and its surrounding seas, down to magnitudes the global catalogues do not carry.

The archive is rebuilt offline: the two global catalogues are merged, duplicate reports of the same event are removed, and the result is compressed into the files the map reads. Because the live window is refetched rather than appended, corrections land here too: a magnitude that the USGS revises from 6.9 to 7.1 changes on the map, and an event later withdrawn disappears.

Magnitudes are revised in the first hours after an earthquake, as more stations report and analysts replace the automatic solution. A difference of a few tenths between this map and a news report, or between two agencies, is normal and is not an error in either.

What the catalogue cannot show

A catalogue records what instruments detect. Worldwide, detection is complete only from about magnitude 4 to 4.5; below that, an earthquake is listed only where a dense regional network exists. That is why California, Japan, Italy and New Zealand look busier than Indonesia or the Andes at small magnitudes. The difference is seismometers, not danger. Compare regions at M 4.5 and above and the picture becomes fair.

The same applies through time. Before the 1960s only large earthquakes were located with any reliability, so a 1930s year with few dots was not a quiet year; it was a poorly observed one. The rise in small events over the decades records the growth of monitoring, not of seismicity.

Using the controls

The period menu spans 24 hours to the whole catalogue, and the two date fields accept any custom range. The timeline under the map scrubs through the selected window; press play to watch a sequence unfold, such as the aftershocks that follow a great earthquake for months. Layers add coastlines, plate boundaries, active faults from the GEM Global Active Faults database and Holocene volcanoes from the Smithsonian Global Volcanism Program. Filters narrow the view by magnitude and depth, and the same filters drive the event table beside the map.

Switch between 2D, a flat map with a choice of basemaps, and 3D, a globe that renders depth. The Japan region opens a box view where depth is drawn downward to scale.

Common questions

I felt an earthquake and it is not here
Either it is below the threshold the local network publishes to the global catalogue, or it has not been published yet. Check your national agency first; for Japan the JMA feed on this map carries far smaller events than the global one.
Why did the magnitude change?
The first magnitude is automatic and uses the nearest stations. Within hours a seismologist reviews it, and for larger events a moment magnitude (Mw) computed from the whole waveform replaces the early estimate. The map shows the latest published value.
Does this map predict earthquakes?
No, and neither does anything else. Seismology can estimate long-term probabilities and the likelihood of aftershocks after a large event, but not the time and place of the next earthquake. For warnings, follow your national agency.
Can I reuse a screenshot or the data?
Yes, with credit; the data use policy gives the credit line and the terms of each source agency.

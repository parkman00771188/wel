How to read these numbers

Everything above is calculated from the same catalogue the live map draws: USGS ANSS ComCat and the ISC Bulletin merged into one record since 1900, with the last 14 days refreshed every ten minutes. Change the period and every figure is recomputed for that window.

The five tiles

Earthquakes
How many catalogued events of magnitude 2.0 and above fall in the selected window, worldwide. The trend compares it with the window of the same length immediately before, so "vs previous period" on a 7-day view means the seven days before that.
Total Magnitude
The plain sum of all magnitudes in the window. It is a rough activity index that moves with the number of events; it is not energy. Energy is dominated by the largest event, since each magnitude unit is about 32 times more of it.
Largest Event
The strongest earthquake in the window and where it was. Over 20 years this is the 2011 Tōhoku earthquake off Honshu, M 9.1.
Damaging range (M 5+)
How many events reached magnitude 5, roughly the size from which shaking damages ordinary buildings near the epicenter. Whether damage actually occurs depends on depth, distance and construction.
Archive
The size of the whole catalogue behind the site, all periods together.

The charts

Earthquakes Over Time bins the window by hour for 24 hours, by day up to 120 days and by month beyond that. The spikes on long views are aftershock sequences: the largest column in the 20-year view is March 2011, when the Tōhoku earthquake was followed by thousands of aftershocks within weeks.

Magnitude Distribution shows how thoroughly small earthquakes outnumber large ones. For every step down in magnitude there are roughly ten times more events, a regularity known as the Gutenberg–Richter law. The M 2–3.9 bands dominate every window; M 7 and above is a fraction of a percent.

Recent Significant Earthquakes lists the newest events of magnitude 4 and above in the window, with depth, in your chosen time zone.

Global Seismic Activity draws each event as a dot for windows up to 120 days. Over longer windows it shows the density of magnitude 4+ earthquakes, so that the plate boundaries appear as bands rather than as a lattice of the largest events, and marks only the great earthquakes individually.

Three things to keep in mind

Counts depend on instruments. The catalogue is complete worldwide only from about magnitude 4 to 4.5. Regions with dense seismometer networks contribute many more small events, and the number of small events grows over the decades as monitoring improves. A rising count of M 2–3 earthquakes since 1980 is a record of better networks, not of a restless planet. Compare periods at M 4.5 and above for a fair picture.

The last few days are provisional. Magnitudes and locations are revised for hours after an event, and the ISC's reviewed solutions arrive about two years later. The dashboard always shows the latest published values, which is why a figure can change slightly between visits.

Regions only apply to short windows. The region menu works on the detailed recent record (up to 120 days). Longer windows are computed over the whole catalogue and are always global.

This dashboard is an overview, not an alert system. For warnings, follow your national agency. For the statistics behind these charts, including the magnitude–frequency curve and energy release over any window, see Seismic Insights; for what magnitude and depth mean, see the Earthquake Guide.
